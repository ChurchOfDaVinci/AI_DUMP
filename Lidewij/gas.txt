 Introduction
This document provides the calculations used to assess the explosion hazards caused by flammable gases inside the enclosure. The battery system uses lithium iron phosphate (LFP) cells in modules, which can enter thermal runaway in case of severe overheating. During thermal runaway, several gases may be released into the enclosure. It is therefore necessary to verify that the enclosure can safely handle these gas releases.
The explosion hazard has two aspects. First, the released gases increase the pressure inside the enclosure. It must be determined whether the enclosure can withstand this pressure or whether the available openings provide enough venting capacity to prevent excessive pressure buildup.
Second, the composition of the released gases must be evaluated to ensure that the concentration of flammable gases remains below the lower explosive limit (LEL). The design target is to keep the concentration below 25% of the LEL.

Pressure building from the battery venting in e-PU10 enclosure (without ventilation)
In a sealed enclosure the increase of gas volume will result in an increase in pressure:
PV=nRT
P = pressure of the gas (Pa)
V = volume of the gas (L)
n = amount of gas (mol)
T = temperature (K)
R = gas constant = 8.314 J/(mol K)
As the V, R and T stay constant, n increases and therefore P increases.
The pressure will increase by the venting of gases:
P_2=P_1*(V_1/V_2 )
P1 = pressure in situation 1
P2 = pressure in situation 2
V1 = volume in situation 1
V2 = volume in situation 2
For these calculations we assume that the gases are ideal gases. We also assume that the gases that are vented are incompressible.
We calculate the worst case, therefore we assume that all the gases have the high venting flow rate of hydrogen of a 130 L/min (see figure 5) and because it is 43,066% of the gases.
To calculate the total volume of 100% of the gases:
V_(totaal gas)=(V_Hydrogen*100%)/(43.066%)=302 L/min
As we will calculate the worst case scenario, we will use the flow rate and volume of THC.
Calculating the pressure after one minute
P_2= P_1*(V_1/V_2 )=P_1*(V_1/(V_1-V_(totaal gas) ))=1*8067/7765=1,038 bar= 10380 Pa

 Pressure relief from the enclosure
In a sealed enclosure the increase of gas volume will result in an increase in pressure. The added pressure by the vented gases is 1.038 in the first minute (See chapter 3.2).
The enclosure is IP55 and not completely sealed. There are four openings of 5x50mm2 in the corners of the enclosure. See figure 4. Through this opening the pressure can be released. 
 
Figure 6 Opening in enclosure e-PU10
The pressure difference between inside and outside the enclosure is:
ΔP=P_2-P_1=0.038 bar=3800 Pa
This means that we don’t have a “choked” flow and we don’t have compressibility effects. Therefore, we can use the following formula (Bernoulli):
Q=C_d  A √((2 ΔP)/ρ)
Q =  	flow through the opening (m3/s)
Cd = 	0.7 (realistic value for air)
A = 		area of the opening = 4*250 mm2 = 4*250*10-6 m2
ΔP = 	difference in pressure (Pa)
ρ = 		density (of air =1.2 kg/m3)
Q=C_d  A √((2 ΔP)/ρ)=0,055 m^3/s=0,055*60*1000  L/min=3342  L/min
Calculate maximum pressure, including ventilation through openings
Here, we will calculate which pressure build up is necessary inside the enclosure to achieve a flow that is the same as the flow that is created by the battery venting.
To calculate the worst case, we will calculate with the gases that have the highest flow rate (THC). 1667 L will be injected per minute during the first minute. To not get an overpressure, a flow of at least 1667 L/min is necessary through the openings.
1667  L/min=0.028 m^3/s
Rewrite to formula from 3.6 to determine the pressure:
Q=C_d  A √((2 ΔP)/ρ)   → ΔP=  1/2 ρ(Q/(C_d*A))^2
ΔP=  1/2 ρ(Q/(C_d*A))^2=  1/2*1.2*(0,028/(0,7*(4*250*10^(-6) ) ))^2=960 Pa
P=100960 Pa=1,0096 bar

This is not very much, and thus the openings in the enclosure are enough to vent so that there will be no overpressure.
 Iterative calculation to determine the flow rate to not reach LEL
The LEL of Hydrogen and the THC must stay below 1.0% and 0.7%, respectively (25% of LEL). To stay on the safe side, we use the lowest LEL of the hydrocarbons (2.7%). To determine if we stay below this limit an iterative calculation is performed.
To calculate the concentration change, we have the following formula:
dC/dt=(G(t))/V-Q/V C(t)
C = 		gas concentration (-)
t = 		time (s)
G = 		gas generation (m3)
V =		container volume (m3)
Q = 		ventilation rate (m3/s)
Rewrite this for an iterative calculation, where we assume the timestep is 1s:
C(t)=C(t-1)+(G(t-1))/V-Q/V C(t-1)

 Conclusion
The calculations show that the existing openings in the enclosure provide sufficient venting capacity to accommodate the gas release during a thermal runaway event. As a result, the pressure increase inside the enclosure remains within acceptable limits and no excessive overpressure is expected.
However, the resulting airflow through these openings is not sufficient to keep the concentration of flammable gases below the design criterion of 25% of the lower explosive limit (LEL). Additional ventilation is therefore required to maintain a safe gas concentration.
Based on the results of this assessment, an active ventilation system providing an additional airflow of 0.5 m³/s is required to ensure that the flammable gas concentration remains below the specified design limit.
